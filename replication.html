Local Heterogeneity in Artificial Intelligence Jobs Over Time and Space

A Replication Study of Andreadis et al. (AEA Papers and Proceedings, 2025)

Authors

Jacob Khaykin5

David Kane6

JEL: J24, O33, R11

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Regional Economics, Labor Markets

Data Availability: The R code and data to reproduce this replication are available in this repository: https://github.com/JacobKhay/Andreadis-Replication.

Abstract

We partially replicate @andreadis2025 on the correlation between the level and growth of artificial intelligence employment across U.S. counties from 2014 to 2023 and a collection of variables related to education, innovation, and industry. We successfully reproduce most of their results and, to assess robustness, extend their analysis by using log-population weights. Most, but not all, of the associations persist. We critique the construction of a key variable and the authors’ unsupported causal claims.

Introduction

This paper replicates @andreadis2025, henceforth AKCLM, on the correlation between the level and growth of artificial intelligence employment and education, innovation, and industry factors across U.S. counties from 2014 to 2023. Understanding where AI intensity emerges and how it spreads matters for researchers and policymakers alike: AI has the potential to reshape regional economies [@acemoglu2024; @brynjolfsson2021], alter the demand for skills [@autor2015; @acemoglu2019], and shift patterns of innovation [@babina2024].

AKCLM define “AI intensity” as AI-related job postings divided by total job postings in US counties. There are two outcome variables: the level of AI intensity for each county-year and the growth of AI intensity between 2017-2018 and 2022-2023 for each county.

AKCLM link county-level AI intensity to several explanatory factors. Education is captured by the share of adults with a college degree, innovation by local patenting activity [@giczy2022], and industry by the composition of employment across sectors.

AKCLM find that both the level and growth of AI intensity are associated with higher educational attainment, more innovation activity, and certain industry profiles. For the level of AI intensity, bachelor’s degree share, and STEM degree share are positively associated, while manufacturing intensity is negatively associated. For the growth of AI intensity and STEM degree share remain significantly correlated, while turnover rate shows a negative association.

We reproduce these results and extend the analysis using log-population weights. Under this adjustment, the core associations persist for both outcomes but estimated magnitudes generally shrink. For the level of AI intensity, patents per employee shows the most robust association across weighting schemes, while bachelor’s share shows the largest magnitude decline. For the growth of AI intensity, STEM degree share show the most consistent associations, though effect sizes diminish when we weight by log-population.

Finally, AKCLM’s causal interpretations are not supported by the observational nature of their data [@holland1986].

Data

AKCLM utilize multiple data sources to construct a comprehensive county-level dataset spanning 2014–2023:

AI Job Employment Data: Lightcast [@beckett2023] aggregates job postings from over 40,000 online job boards, newspapers, and employer websites. AI-related jobs are identified through skills and keywords associated with AI development and use [@acemoglu2022]. The first dependent variable, AI intensity, is defined as:

\[ AI_{it} = \frac{\text{AI job postings}_{it}}{\text{Total job postings}_{it}} \times 100 \tag{1} \]

AI intensity has a mean of 0.4 percentage points and a standard deviation of 1.1 percentage points across county-year observations.

The second dependent variable captures the percentage point change in AI intensity between the 2017-2018 average and the 2022-2023 average:

\[ \Delta AI_{i} = \frac{\sum_{t \in \{2022,2023\}} \text{AI}_{it}}{\sum_{t \in \{2022,2023\}} \text{Total}_{it}} - \frac{\sum_{t \in \{2017,2018\}} \text{AI}_{it}}{\sum_{t \in \{2017,2018\}} \text{Total}_{it}} \tag{2} \]

The change in AI intensity has a mean of 0.3 percentage points and a standard deviation of 1.0 percentage points across counties.

Demographic Variables: From the American Community Survey [@census_acs], AKCLM construct several county-level demographic measures, including bachelor’s share (percentage of workforce with bachelor’s degree or higher), black population share (percentage of county population identifying as Black), poverty share (percentage of population below federal poverty line), log-population (natural logarithm of county population), and log median income (natural logarithm of median household income).

Innovation Indicators: AKCLM construct several innovation measures, including patents per employee (USPTO patent counts normalized by employment), AI patents share (percentage of patents classified as AI-related) [@giczy2022], STEM degrees share (percentage of awarded degrees in STEM fields), and degrees per capita (total degrees awarded per capita).

Industry and Labor Market Variables: AKCLM construct several industry and labor market measures, including labor market tightness (ratio of job postings to unemployed workers) [@bls_laus], manufacturing intensity (employment share in manufacturing sector) [@census_cbp], ICT intensity (employment share in information and communication technology), turnover rate (worker separation rate from Quarterly Workforce Indicators) [@census_qwi], and large establishments share (percentage of employment in large firms). Housing Market: AKCLM include house price growth from the Federal Housing Finance Agency [@bogin2019].

Housing Market: We include house price growth from Federal Housing Finance Agency [@bogin2019].

All explanatory variables are lagged by one year.

An important statistical issue arises from the construction of labor market tightness. AI intensity is defined as AI job postings divided by total job postings, while labor market tightness is constructed as total job postings divided by unemployed workers. Total job postings thus appears in the denominator of the dependent variable and in the numerator of a key regressor.

This creates denominator contamination, or mechanical endogeneity: both sides of the regression share the same underlying quantity, inducing a correlation that exists purely through algebraic construction.

\[ \frac{\text{AI job postings}_{it}}{\text{Total job postings}_{it}} = \alpha + \beta \frac{\text{Total job postings}_{it}}{\text{Unemployed}_{it}} + X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{3} \]

This can bias coefficient estimates and overstate statistical significance. The issue is analogous to regressing \(Y/Z\) on \(Z/X\) where the shared \(Z\) induces correlation even when \(Y\) and \(X\) are actually independent. Kronmal [-@kronmal1993spurious] shows that when the dependent variable is a ratio sharing a component with an independent variable, “regression coefficient estimates for the other independent variables … result in inappropriate conclusions.”

Replication

We use R [@ihaka1996r] and the tidyverse collection of packages [@tidyverse]. To estimate and display regression models, we use fixest [@fixest] and modelsummary [@modelsummary].

Following AKCLM, we report only variables that have statistically significant coefficients in at least one model specification.

Table 1

The core model for the level of AI intensity is a two-way fixed-effects regression:

\[ AI_{it} = \alpha_i + \alpha_t + \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}_{it-1} + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{4} \]

where \(\alpha_i\) and \(\alpha_t\) are county and year fixed effects, \(\mathbf{X}_{it-1}\) is a vector of standardized regressors lagged one year, and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is an error term clustered at the county level.

We reproduce Table 1 from AKCLM. All coefficients and standard errors match the original results.

Table 1 presents parameter estimates for five models, each using AI intensity as the outcome, which has a mean of 0.4 percentage points and a standard deviation of 1.1 percentage points across county-year observations. All regressors are standardized. Column 4 (the preferred specification) includes the full control set with county and year fixed effects; all subsequent discussion refers to this column.

Bachelor’s share (0.18, p < 0.001) is the largest demographic predictor of AI intensity. Two counties differing by one standard deviation in bachelor’s share are expected to differ by 0.18 percentage points in AI intensity, which is 42% of the mean and 16% of the standard deviation.

Among the innovation variables, STEM degree share (0.048, p < 0.05) carries a coefficient 62% larger than patents per employee (0.029, p < 0.01). Two counties differing by one standard deviation in STEM degree share are expected to differ by 0.048 percentage points in AI intensity (11% of the mean and 4% of the standard deviation), compared with 0.029 percentage points for patents per employee (7% of the mean, 3% of the standard deviation).

Among industry variables, ICT intensity (0.026, p = 0.058) and manufacturing intensity (−0.033, p < 0.01) exhibit similar effect sizes but opposite signs. Two counties differing by one standard deviation in manufacturing intensity are expected to differ by 0.033 percentage points (8% of the standard deviation), compared with 0.026 percentage points for ICT intensity (6% of the standard deviation).

Table 2

For the growth of AI intensity, we estimate a cross-sectional OLS regression with no fixed effects:

\[ \Delta AI_{i} = \beta_0 + \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}_{i,2017} + \varepsilon_{i} \tag{5} \]

where \(\Delta AI_{i}\) is the percentage point change in AI intensity between the 2017–2018 and 2022–2023 averages, \(\beta_0\) is a constant term representing expected AI intensity growth when all predictors are at their mean, \(\mathbf{X}_{i,2017}\) is a vector of standardized regressors measured in 2017, and \(\varepsilon_{i}\) is an error term clustered at the county level. Unlike equation (3), there is no time subscript \(t\) because the growth of AI intensity is a single observation per county — the change between two pooled periods — rather than a panel. This model does not include county or year fixed effects, as the outcome is already a first difference across counties.

We reproduce Table 2 from AKCLM. All our results match AKCLM’s, except for coefficient and standard error for bachelor’s share in all the models in which it appears. The source of this discrepancy is unclear; the authors were contacted but did not respond.

Table 2 analyzes predictors of the growth of AI intensity between 2017–2018 and 2022–2023. All regressors are standardized, with explanatory variables measured in 2017. Column 4 includes the full control set and serves as the preferred specification; all subsequent discussion refers to this column.

STEM degree share (0.054, p < 0.01) is the largest statistically significant predictor of AI intensity growth in the preferred specification. Two counties differing by one standard deviation in STEM degree share are expected to differ by 0.054 percentage points in AI intensity growth, equivalent to 61% of the median of 0.088 percentage points.

Among labor market variables, turnover rate (−0.042, p < 0.01) carries a coefficient 2.7 times larger in magnitude than large establishment share (−0.016, p = 0.47). Two counties differing by one standard deviation in turnover rate are expected to differ by 0.042 percentage points in AI intensity growth (48% of the median), compared with 0.016 percentage points for large establishment share (18% of the median).

We assess robustness by re-estimating AKCLM’s preferred specifications weighted by log-population. Of the 5 coefficients that are statistically significant at the 5% level under equal weighting across both dependent variables, only 3 remain significant under log-population weighting. For the level of AI intensity, bachelor’s share and STEM degree share remain significant, while patents per employee and manufacturing intensity lose significance. For the growth of AI intensity, STEM degree share remains significant under both weighting schemes.

Extension: Log-Population Weighting Analysis

To assess robustness, we re-estimate all models using log-population weights, omitting labor market tightness due to the denominator contamination problem described above. Log-population weights give more weight to populous counties relative to equal weighting, where each county receives the same weight regardless of population size.

In the fully specified Model 4, bachelor’s share shows a small negative association with the growth of AI intensity under both weighting schemes, suggesting that counties with higher educational attainment in 2017 already had higher baseline AI intensity, leaving less room for growth. House price growth shows minimal independent effect. Among innovation variables, patents per employee declines from 0.06 percentage points under equal weights to 0.04 under log-population weights (a 33% reduction), while STEM degree share declines from 0.08 to 0.05 percentage points (a 38% reduction). Among industry variables, manufacturing intensity shows a negative association of approximately 0.03–0.04 percentage points, ICT intensity declines from 0.12 to 0.07 percentage points (a 42% reduction), and turnover rate declines from 0.17–0.18 to 0.11 percentage points (a 39% reduction).

Of the coefficients highlighted by AKCLM in their preferred levels specification, most decrease in absolute value under log-population weighting. STEM degree share shows the most stability across weighting schemes, while bachelor’s share, turnover rate, and manufacturing intensity all weaken under population weighting, suggesting these relationships are stronger in smaller counties. ICT intensity and patents per employee are exceptions, becoming slightly larger under population weighting. Overall, the direction of all associations is preserved, but the pattern of magnitude shifts suggests that AI intensity correlates more strongly with education and labor market structure in smaller counties.

AKCLM employ language suggesting causal relationships despite using observational county-level data with fixed-effects regressions. The empirical strategy does not support causal inference [@holland1986], yet the paper uses terminology that implies causation rather than correlation. Causal nouns such as “drivers,” “determinants,” “effects,” and “impact” appear throughout, alongside causal verbs such as “drive,” “fostering,” and “influence.” For instance, the paper refers to “key drivers of AI job intensity” and states that labor market tightness “emerges as a key driver,” implying that changing these variables would directly alter AI adoption. The conclusion states that findings “point to the role of place-based policies,” making policy recommendations based on associations rather than identified causal effects.

Conclusion

This replication of AKCLM confirms that education, innovation, and industry factors are correlated with AI intensity across U.S. counties. Our analysis highlights three qualifications to their conclusions.

First, AKCLM employ causal language that overstates what the empirical design can support. Terms such as “drivers” and “determinants” suggest causal mechanisms, but simple regressions can only document conditional correlations, in the absence of an explicit identification strategy [@holland1986],

Second, the construction of labor market tightness (total job postings divided by unemployed workers) introduces a mechanical correlation with AI intensity, which has total job postings in its denominator. Any change in total postings simultaneously raises tightness and lowers measured AI intensity, biasing the estimated coefficient. AKCLM do not acknowledge this issue.

Third, several associations are sensitive to the weighting scheme. ICT intensity shows the largest magnitude shift, declining by approximately 42% under log-population weights, while patents per employee, STEM degree share, and turnover rate all decline by roughly one-third.
