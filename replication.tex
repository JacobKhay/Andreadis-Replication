\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

We partially replicate Andreadis et al.
(\citeproc{ref-andreadis2025}{2025}) on the correlation between the
level and growth of artificial intelligence employment across U.S.
counties from 2014 to 2023 and a collection of variables related to
education, innovation, and industry. We successfully reproduce most of
their results and, to assess robustness, extend their analysis by using
log-population weights. Only some of the associations persist. We
critique the construction of a key variable and their unsupported causal
claims.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

This paper replicates Andreadis et al.
(\citeproc{ref-andreadis2025}{2025}), henceforth AKCLM, on the
correlation between the level and growth of artificial intelligence
employment and education, innovation, and industry factors across U.S.
counties from 2014 to 2023. Understanding where AI intensity emerges and
how it spreads matters for researchers and policymakers alike: AI has
the potential to reshape regional economies
(\citeproc{ref-acemoglu2024}{Acemoglu, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-brynjolfsson2021}{Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson,
2021}), alter the demand for skills
(\citeproc{ref-acemoglu2019}{Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019};
\citeproc{ref-autor2015}{Autor, 2015}), and shift patterns of innovation
(\citeproc{ref-babina2024}{Babina et al., 2024}). County-level variation
offers a granular view of how these transformations unfold across the
United States.

There are two outcome variables, both measures of AI intensity --- the
share of AI-related job postings in total job postings at the
county-year level. The first is the level of AI intensity. The second is
the percentage point change in AI intensity between 2017--2018 and
2022--2023. Together, these outcomes distinguish between where AI jobs
are concentrated and where AI adoption is accelerating most rapidly.
Tracking both provides insight into which regions gain early access to
AI intensity and which experience the fastest growth.

AKCLM link county-level AI intensity to several explanatory factors.
Education is captured by the share of adults with a college degree,
innovation by local patenting activity (\citeproc{ref-giczy2022}{Giczy,
Pairolero, and Toole, 2022}), and industry by the composition of
employment across sectors. Together, these variables represent
structural characteristics that may determine whether a region becomes a
hub for AI employment.

AKCLM find that both the level and growth of AI intensity are associated
with higher educational attainment, more innovation activity, and
certain industry profiles. For the level of AI intensity, bachelor's
degree share, labor market tightness, and STEM degree share are
positively associated, while manufacturing intensity is negatively
associated. For the growth of AI intensity, STEM degree share and labor
market tightness remain significantly correlated, while turnover rate
shows a negative association. These associations are robust across
specifications, suggesting that counties with strong human capital,
innovative capacity, and aligned industries experience both higher AI
intensity and faster AI adoption. The results highlight structural
divides in access to AI employment across the country.

We reproduce these results and extend the analysis using log-population
weights. Under this adjustment, the core associations persist for both
outcomes but estimated magnitudes generally shrink. For the level of AI
intensity, labor market tightness shows the most robust association
across weighting schemes, while bachelor's share shows the largest
magnitude decline. For the growth of AI intensity, STEM degree share and
labor market tightness show the most consistent associations, though
effect sizes diminish when larger counties receive less weight.

Finally, AKCLM's causal interpretations are not supported by the
observational nature of their data (\citeproc{ref-holland1986}{Holland,
1986}). The associations documented do not establish that education,
innovation, or industry factors directly cause higher AI intensity. Our
replication underscores the value of the evidence while emphasizing the
limits of what can be inferred from the design.

\section{Data}\label{data}

AKCLM utilize multiple data sources to construct a comprehensive
county-level dataset spanning 2014--2023:

\emph{AI Job Employment Data}: Job posting data from Lightcast
(\citeproc{ref-beckett2023}{Beckett, 2023}), which aggregates
information from over 40,000 online job boards, newspapers, and employer
websites. AI-related jobs are identified through skills and keywords
associated with AI development and use
(\citeproc{ref-acemoglu2022}{Acemoglu et al., 2022}). The first
dependent variable, AI Intensity \(AI_{it}\), is defined as:

\[
AI_{it} = \frac{\text{AI job postings}_{it}}{\text{Total job postings}_{it}} \times 100 \tag{1}
\]

The second dependent variable captures the percentage point change in AI
Intensity between the 2017-2018 average and the 2022-2023 average:
\(\Delta AI_i = \overline{AI}_{i,2022-2023} - \overline{AI}_{i,2017-2018}\).

\emph{Demographic Variables}: From the American Community Survey
(\citeproc{ref-census_acs}{U.S. Census Bureau, 2024a}), we include
bachelor's share (percentage of workforce with bachelor's degree or
higher), black population share (percentage of county population
identifying as Black), poverty share (percentage of population below
federal poverty line), log population (natural logarithm of county
population), and log median income (natural logarithm of median
household income).

\emph{Innovation Indicators}: We measure patents per employee (USPTO
patent counts normalized by employment), AI patents share (percentage of
patents classified as AI-related) (\citeproc{ref-giczy2022}{Giczy,
Pairolero, and Toole, 2022}), STEM degrees share (percentage of awarded
degrees in STEM fields), and degrees per capita (total degrees awarded
per capita).

\emph{Industry and Labor Market Variables}: These include labor market
tightness (ratio of job postings to unemployed workers)
(\citeproc{ref-bls_laus}{U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024}),
manufacturing intensity (employment share in manufacturing sector)
(\citeproc{ref-census_cbp}{U.S. Census Bureau, 2024b}), ICT intensity
(employment share in information and communication technology), turnover
rate (worker separation rate from Quarterly Workforce Indicators)
(\citeproc{ref-census_qwi}{U.S. Census Bureau, 2024c}), and large
establishments share (percentage of employment in large firms).

\emph{Housing Market}: We include house price growth from Federal
Housing Finance Agency (\citeproc{ref-bogin2019}{Bogin, Doerner, and
Larson, 2019}).

All explanatory variables are lagged by one year. A significant
statistical issue arises from the construction of the variables. AI
intensity is defined as AI job postings divided by total job postings.
Labor market tightness, one of the key independent variables, is
constructed as total job postings divided by unemployed workers. Thus,
total job postings appears in the denominator of the dependent variable
and in the numerator of one of the independent variables.

This creates denominator contamination, or mechanical endogeneity: both
sides of the regression share the same underlying quantity, inducing a
correlation that exists purely through algebraic construction. \[
\frac{AIJobs_{it}}{TotalJobs_{it}} = \alpha + \beta \frac{TotalJobs_{it}}{Unemployed_{it}} + X'_{it}\gamma + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{2}
\]

This can bias coefficient estimates and overstate statistical
significance. The issue is analogous to regressing \(Y/Z\) on \(Z/X\)
where the shared \(Z\) induces correlation even when \(Y\) and \(X\) are
actually independent. Kronmal (\citeproc{ref-kronmal1993spurious}{1993})
shows that when the dependent variable is a ratio sharing a component
with an independent variable, ``regression coefficient estimates for the
other independent variables\ldots{} result in inappropriate
conclusions.''

\section{Replication}\label{replication}

We use R (\citeproc{ref-ihaka1996r}{Ihaka and Gentleman, 1996}), a free
and open-source statistical computing environment, and the tidyverse
collection of packages (\citeproc{ref-tidyverse}{Wickham et al., 2019}).
To estimate and display regression models, we use the fixest
(\citeproc{ref-fixest}{Bergé, 2018}) and modelsummary
(\citeproc{ref-modelsummary}{Arel-Bundock, 2022}) packages.

The core model we estimate for the level of AI intensity is a two-way
fixed-effects regression:

\[\text{AI Intensity}_{it} = \alpha_i + \alpha_t + \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}_{it-1} + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{3}\]

where \(\alpha_i\) and \(\alpha_t\) are county and year fixed effects,
\(\mathbf{X}_{it-1}\) is a vector of standardized regressors lagged one
year (including bachelor's share, labor market tightness, STEM degree
share, patents per employee, AI patent share, manufacturing intensity,
ICT intensity, turnover rate, and demographic controls), and
\(\varepsilon_{it}\) is an error term clustered at the county level. For
the growth outcome, we estimate the same specification without county
and year fixed effects, replacing \(\text{AI Intensity}_{it}\) with the
percentage point change in AI Intensity between 2017--2018 and
2022--2023.

We reproduce Table 1, Table 2, and Figure 1 from AKCLM. Nearly all
coefficients and standard errors match the original results. However,
the coefficient and standard error for bachelor's share are inconsistent
with the results published in Table 2. We cannot reproduce the maps in
Figure 1, in part because AKCLM's data does not include values for
counties in Connecticut.

\subsection{Table 1}\label{table-1}

Table 1 presents parameter estimates for five models, each using AI
intensity as the outcome. All regressors are standardized. Column 4 ---
the preferred specification --- includes the full control set with
county and year fixed effects; all subsequent discussion refers to this
column.

Labor market tightness (0.28, p \textless{} 0.001) and bachelor's share
(0.18, p \textless{} 0.001) are the two largest predictors of AI
intensity. Tightness carries a coefficient 52\% larger than bachelor's
share. A one-standard-deviation increase in tightness corresponds to
0.095 percentage points --- 61\% of the sample mean of 0.45\% ---
compared with 40\% for bachelor's share. Both coefficients are
significant at the 0.1\% level. Tightness thus exhibits both a larger
point estimate and greater practical magnitude.

Within the innovation variables, STEM degree share (0.048, p \textless{}
0.05) carries a coefficient 62\% larger than patents per employee
(0.029, p \textless{} 0.01). A one-standard-deviation difference in STEM
degree share corresponds to 11\% of mean AI intensity, compared with 7\%
for patents per employee. Statistical precision favors patents,
significant at the 1\% level versus 5\% for STEM degree share. STEM
degree share thus exhibits larger magnitude while patents per employee
exhibits greater precision.

Among industry variables, ICT intensity (0.026, p = 0.058) and
manufacturing intensity (−0.033, p \textless{} 0.01) exhibit similar
effect sizes but opposite signs. Manufacturing shows a 30\% larger
effect size than ICT intensity (0.033 vs.~0.026) and is significant at
the 1\% level, whereas ICT intensity is significant only at the 10\%
level. Manufacturing thus exhibits both a larger point estimate and
greater precision.

Comparing education variables, bachelor's share (0.18, p \textless{}
0.001) carries a coefficient 3.8 times larger than STEM degree share
(0.048, p \textless{} 0.05). A one-standard-deviation difference in
bachelor's share corresponds to 0.13 percentage points --- 40\% of mean
AI intensity --- compared with 11\% for STEM degree share. Bachelor's
share is also more precisely estimated, significant at the 0.1\% level
versus 5\% for STEM degree share. Bachelor's share thus dominates on
both magnitude and statistical precision.

\subsection{Table 2}\label{table-2}

Table 2 analyzes predictors of the growth of AI intensity between
2017--2018 and 2022--2023. All regressors are standardized, with
explanatory variables measured in 2017. Column 4 includes the full
control set and serves as the preferred specification; all subsequent
discussion refers to this column.

Labor market tightness (0.073, p \textless{} 0.01) carries a coefficient
36\% larger than STEM degree share (0.054, p \textless{} 0.001). A
one-standard-deviation increase in tightness corresponds to 0.019 more
percentage points of growth than an equivalent shift in STEM degree
share. Relative to median growth of 0.088 percentage points, tightness
accounts for 83\% and STEM degree share for 61\%. Both coefficients are
significant at the 1\% level. Tightness thus exhibits a larger point
estimate, though the gap is considerably narrower than in the levels
specification of Table 1.

Among labor market variables, turnover rate (−0.042, p = 0.072) carries
a coefficient 2.7 times larger in magnitude than large establishment
share (−0.016, p = 0.47). A one-standard-deviation difference in
turnover rate corresponds to 48\% of median growth, compared with 18\%
for large establishment share. Turnover rate approaches significance at
the 10\% level, while large establishment share shows no statistical
evidence. Turnover rate thus exhibits both larger magnitude and greater
precision, though neither estimate is precisely measured.

Among industry variables, manufacturing intensity (−0.013, p = 0.48) and
ICT intensity (0.014, p = 0.42) show nearly identical effect sizes but
opposite signs. Neither coefficient approaches statistical significance.
This contrasts sharply with the levels specification of Table 1, where
manufacturing intensity is significant at the 1\% level with a
coefficient of −0.033. Both effect sizes and statistical precision
collapse in the growth specification of Table 2.

\subsection{Results}\label{results}

The comparison between equal-weighted and log-population-weighted
regressions reveals several patterns. For the level of AI intensity,
bachelor's share decreases by approximately 25\% under log-population
weights (from 0.227 to 0.182), suggesting that the relationship between
educational attainment and AI intensity is stronger in smaller counties.
ICT intensity shows the largest increase, growing by approximately 53\%
under log-population weights (from 0.012 to 0.026). Patents per employee
and manufacturing intensity grow by approximately 17\% and 21\%
respectively, while turnover rate decreases by approximately 39\% (from
0.026 to 0.019). STEM degree share shows the most stability, changing by
only 13\%. Labor market tightness is omitted from the log-population
weighted models due to denominator contamination. Overall, the direction
of associations is preserved across weighting schemes, but the
magnitudes shift in ways that suggest some relationships are stronger in
larger counties while others are stronger in smaller counties.

\section{Critical Assessment of Causal
Language}\label{critical-assessment-of-causal-language}

AKCLM employ language suggesting causal relationships despite using
observational county-level data with fixed-effects regressions. The
empirical strategy does not support causal inference
(\citeproc{ref-holland1986}{Holland, 1986}), yet the paper uses
terminology that implies causation rather than correlation. Causal nouns
such as ``drivers,'' ``determinants,'' ``effects,'' and ``impact''
appear throughout, alongside causal verbs such as ``drive,''
``fostering,'' and ``influence.'' For instance, the paper refers to
``key drivers of AI job intensity'' and states that labor market
tightness ``emerges as a key driver,'' implying that changing these
variables would directly alter AI adoption. The conclusion states that
findings ``point to the role of place-based policies,'' making policy
recommendations based on associations rather than identified causal
effects. The fixed-effects specification controls for time-invariant
county characteristics and common time trends, but cannot address
reverse causality, omitted time-varying confounds, or selection
processes (\citeproc{ref-holland1986}{Holland, 1986}).

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

This replication of AKCLM confirms that local labor market conditions,
human capital, and innovation capacity are correlated with AI intensity
across U.S. counties. Our analysis highlights three qualifications to
their conclusions. First, AKCLM employ causal language that overstates
what the empirical design can support. Terms such as ``drivers'' and
``determinants'' suggest causal mechanisms, but fixed-effects
regressions can only document conditional correlations. Without
exogenous variation or quasi-experimental identification
(\citeproc{ref-holland1986}{Holland, 1986}), these patterns likely
reflect some combination of causal effects, reverse causality, and
selection.

Second, several associations are sensitive to the weighting scheme.
Labor market tightness is the most stable correlate across both outcomes
and both weighting schemes. By contrast, ICT intensity shows the largest
magnitude shift, declining by approximately 53\% under log-population
weights, while bachelor's share increases by approximately 25\%,
suggesting these relationships are not uniform across the county size
distribution.

Third, the direction of all associations is preserved across weighting
schemes, indicating that the core findings of AKCLM are reproducible.
However, the magnitudes shift enough to caution against treating point
estimates from the equal-weighted specification as representative of all
counties.

More broadly, this replication underscores that modest changes in
specification, such as alternative weighting schemes, can materially
shift the interpretation of empirical results on technological change.
